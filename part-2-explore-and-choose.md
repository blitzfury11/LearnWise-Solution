# Part 2: Explore and choose a direction

## Option A and Option B

Option A. Improve the written conversation. No microphone is involved. Mrs Jansen responds in one or two sentences, the brief stays visible throughout the conversation, and students can still choose which attempt to submit. The grade focuses on written communication. Law remains text-based, and students get the same experience whether they are studying on a train or sitting at a desk.

Option B. Let the skill determine the interaction mode. The institution can choose to enable speaking, giving students three ways to complete an attempt:

- Speak throughout the entire conversation.
- Type throughout the entire conversation.
- Switch between speaking and typing at any point, without starting a new attempt.

Typing is always available for students who cannot speak in their current environment. It is not treated as a fallback after voice. Students who speak through the final turn have completed their attempt, just like those who type.

The instructor defines whether the exercise assesses precise writing or spoken conversation. Based on that choice, the system suggests a mode and briefly explains why. The instructor can still change or reject the suggestion. Grading is based on the transcript, with spoken turns also capturing pauses, such as "12s silence". Once the transcript is ready, the recording is deleted.

## Why Option B

I'd choose Option B.

It builds on the improvements from Option A while also addressing the limitations of text-based interactions. When a student using Option B chooses to type, they still benefit from shorter AI replies, a brief that stays on screen, and a clearer indication of when the attempt is finished.

The research shows that most abandonment happens between turns 3 and 6, while the typical AI reply is around 148 words. These changes help test whether long responses and a disappearing brief are contributing to the problem, without requiring every student to use voice.

However, Option B also allows nursing, counselling, and negotiation exercises to assess more than written communication. Students can complete an attempt by speaking or typing, with timing captured when they speak.

What Option B gives up:

- Voice remains disabled until the university approves a clear explanation of who processes the speech, where it is processed, how long the transcript is stored, and how recordings are deleted. If approval is denied, the institution stays with Option A.
- The transcript captures words and pauses, but not tone of voice. Assessing tone would require keeping recordings and would need a separate admin review.
- Students taking the same course may submit different types of evidence. Spoken turns include timing, while typed turns are marked as "not evidenced" when it comes to pauses.
- Speaking costs more and is more likely to fail. Admins can set a limit on speaking minutes per student. Once that limit is reached, the student can continue the same attempt by typing.

The mode suggestion is based on one question added to the existing admin template: does the exercise assess precise writing or a spoken conversation? This also addresses the concern from Part 1 that a list of skills may not always translate clearly into the right interaction mode.

## The flow

The example follows Option B using the existing Mrs Jansen nursing scenario: 12 turns, 2 attempts. The student is taking a patient history from someone who is anxious and hard of hearing.

Admin. Typing is always enabled and cannot be switched off. Speaking remains disabled until the institution approves the speech-to-text process, including who handles the data, where it is processed, how long transcripts are kept, and confirmation that recordings are deleted. The admin also sets a speaking-minute limit per student. Once that limit is reached, the student continues the same attempt by typing. These settings take priority over instructor preferences.

Instructor. The instructor opens the nursing template and answers one question: is the exercise testing precise writing or a spoken conversation? For a patient history, the system suggests speaking and explains why: "A history is a conversation. A written attempt shows the writing." The instructor can still set typing as the default, but cannot remove it as an option. Before publishing, they try the experience once by speaking and once by typing.

If the instructor selects "precise writing", the system suggests typing instead. This is the same recommendation a law course would receive.

Student. The student opens the assignment through the LMS. Before using the microphone, they are told that they can speak or type, that speech is converted into text, and that the recording is not stored.

They can then complete the attempt in three ways:

- Speak throughout the conversation. The attempt is complete.
- Type throughout the conversation, or choose "I can't talk here". The attempt is complete.
- Switch to typing partway through by selecting "Type instead". The current attempt continues, and the second attempt remains available.

Students can review and correct each spoken response before it counts towards the attempt. Mrs Jansen's replies are short, spoken, and captioned, while the brief remains visible on screen. Once the turn limit is reached or the history is complete, the student sees a clear message confirming that the attempt has finished and been saved. Submitting it is a separate step.

Submission and grading. The student chooses which completed attempt to submit. The transcript then appears in the LMS grading interface, alongside the existing draft comments. Each turn is marked as either spoken or typed, with pauses included where relevant.

If a connection is interrupted, the resulting gap is identified as a system issue and is not counted as the student's pause. The transcript also begins with a note explaining what the record can and cannot show. If every turn was typed, timing is marked as "not evidenced".

The instructor grades the selected attempt and can still open the other completed attempt if they need to compare them.

## Edge cases

The student cannot speak where they are. This could be because they are on a train, in a shared room, have a blocked microphone, or cannot comfortably rely on speech. They can switch to typing without losing the turns they have already completed. One student in the research pack completed an attempt on a train. If switching modes meant using another attempt, they would effectively lose one simply because of where they were studying.

The software misunderstands what they say. This is especially important for Dutch, Spanish, accented English, or students speaking in a second language. Students can review and correct each line before it counts. This matters for the nursing student who knows exactly what to say but types slowly. If they cannot correct a misheard response, they end up being graded on the software's mistakes rather than their own knowledge.

The student switches modes, but the rubric assesses timing. Spoken turns retain their pauses, while typed turns are marked as "not evidenced" for timing. An attempt completed entirely through speaking can therefore be assessed on both words and pauses, while an entirely typed attempt is assessed on the written responses. When students switch between the two, the transcript makes it clear which parts provide evidence for each criterion.
